# Performance measures for world-class manufacturing (Maskell)

## Purpose and principle
A factory run by its monthly accounts is run late, by the wrong numbers:
standard-cost variances, overhead absorption and machine utilisation reward
making stock and hide what the floor can change. Brian Maskell's
*Performance Measurement for World Class Manufacturing* (1991) set out what
replaces them: a small set of measures that come straight from the
manufacturing strategy, are mostly **non-financial**, differ from one plant
or line to another, change as the plant's needs change, are simple enough to
be kept by the people doing the work, feed back fast (an hour, a shift — not
a month), and exist to drive improvement rather than to report on it. The
measures are grouped in six areas — **delivery performance and customer
service · process time · production flexibility · quality · financial ·
social issues** — and each plant chooses, inside them, the few that its
strategy needs. The accounts stay for the accounts; the floor is run by
these.

## Questions it answers
Which measures should a line, a project or a factory watch, and which can it
stop watching? Is a proposed measure a good one — does it come from the
strategy, can the team keep it, does it move fast enough to act on? Why do
the floor's measures and the accounts disagree, and which to believe about
the plant?

## Inputs and minimum conditions
The manufacturing strategy in one sentence (what this plant must be good at
— speed, cost, service, new products); the people who will keep the measure
and the moment they can read it; for each candidate measure its definition,
unit, frequency, who counts it and what decision it serves. A measure with
no decision at the end of it is dropped.

## Procedure and calculation
Test a measure against the characteristics — all of them, not most:
1. **Directly related to the manufacturing strategy.** It moves when the
   strategy is being carried out, and not otherwise.
2. **Non-financial, in the main.** Minutes, packs, days, counts, percentages
   — money is the consequence, worked out from them (`cost.line-cost-model`),
   not the measure the floor keeps.
3. **Varies between locations.** A line's measures are its own; the same
   list everywhere measures nothing.
4. **Changes over time.** A measure that has stopped moving has done its
   work; it is retired and the next weakness measured.
5. **Simple and easy to use.** Counted or timed by the people on the line,
   drawn by hand on a board if need be.
6. **Fast feedback.** Hourly, by shift or daily — never only monthly.
7. **Intended to foster improvement**, not just monitor: it points at what to
   do next.

Then place it in its area and pick the few that matter. The book's example
measures, by area (delivery and process time as its reviewers list them;
the rest as commonly summarised):
- *Delivery and customer service* — vendor delivery performance, production
  schedule adherence, order and schedule changes, customer service level,
  lost-sales analysis.
- *Process time* — manufacturing cycle time, delivery and production lead
  time, set-up times, material availability, distance material moves,
  machine uptime, customer service time.
- *Production flexibility* — the number of products and the mix a line can
  run, set-up time and lot size, the time to introduce a new product, how
  many people can do how many jobs.
- *Quality* — incoming (vendor) quality, in-process yield and first-time-
  through, scrap and rework, customer returns, and the cost of quality.
- *Financial* — the few that still matter on the floor: inventory (turns,
  days), productivity, and cost measured as the direct cost of a value
  stream rather than absorbed overhead.
- *Social* — safety, absenteeism and turnover, suggestions and training,
  the environment.

The book's second half says how: set targets from the strategy (not from
last year), produce the measures where the work is, and — chapter twelve —
what to do about the accounts: activity-based costing and life-cycle costing
as the alternatives to standard costing (Maskell's later work, with Baggaley,
became lean accounting's value stream costing — `cost.inventory-carrying-cost`).

## Assumptions, limits and misuse
- A list copied from the book, or from another plant, fails characteristic 3.
- A monthly KPI pack of thirty numbers fails 5 and 6 whatever is in it.
- Composite indices (OEE as a figure, a "plant score") hide the three or four
  measures inside them and move for several reasons at once — Faultline
  leaves OEE out for this reason (`equipment.oee`).
- The measures are not the accounts' replacement for the company's books;
  they replace the accounts as the way the *floor* is run.
- Written for American plants in 1991 against Japanese competition; the
  measures and the reasoning carry, the examples are of their time.

## Reading the result
"Line 7's strategy is service to two retailers at the lowest cost a pack.
Its measures: schedule adherence by shift (delivery), changeover minutes and
packs in the chiller (process time), first-time-through at the checkweigher
(quality), labour minutes a thousand packs (financial), and days since the
last accident (social). Five, kept on the line's board by the team leader,
read at the shift handover. Machine utilisation is not among them — it
rewards running the depositor when there is no order."

## When not to use it — and instead
Choosing *how* to display and act on the measures day to day →
`visual.visual-management`, `visual.daily-management`. Breaking one target
into the levers that move it → `visual.kpi-tree`. Which product to make
when the constraint is full → `capacity.throughput-accounting`.

## In food and FMCG manufacturing
Retailer service level and case fill, chilled lead time and life on
despatch, changeover minutes, give-away and yield, labour a thousand packs
and days since the last accident are the usual six; the plant's own
strategy picks among them.

## In Faultline
The control room, a line's page and every report keep measures of this
shape without naming the book: counts, minutes and packs read by the people
on the line, each "of" what it should be and "since the record before"
(`visual.reports`); money worked out from them by the one cost model, never
typed as the measure; no OEE. Added 24 October from the books Rowland gave
(`knowledge/SOURCES.md`); the characteristics above are the test a new
measure on a Faultline page should pass.
