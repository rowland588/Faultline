# 1. THE PRODUCT WE ARE BUILDING

Lunar is evolving into an intelligent operational improvement and business intelligence platform.

It has three fundamental components:

1. **The analytical brain** — continuously absorbs information, examines it, identifies patterns, discovers relationships, investigates causes and produces evidence-based findings.
2. **Luna** — the conversational intelligence that understands the business, interprets findings, reasons about problems, communicates naturally and helps people make decisions.
3. **The knowledge infrastructure** — persistent, structured knowledge that connects businesses, sites, departments, production lines, machines, products, people, processes, events, measurements, investigations and documents.

These components must work together.

The analytical engine should not simply produce charts and figures for Luna to read.

It should develop a continuously improving understanding of how a particular business operates.

Luna should then be able to use that understanding naturally in conversation.

The application remains the foundation. We are building intelligence around the application, not building another application around the intelligence.

Preserve the existing product and its capabilities. Do not start again.

# 2. THE MOST IMPORTANT REQUIREMENT: LUNA MUST UNDERSTAND WHY THINGS HAPPEN

This is the most important addition to the previous analytical brief.

Most business reporting tells people what happened.

For example:

- Yesterday's production was 8% below target.
- Waste increased by 2%.
- Line efficiency fell.
- Labour costs increased.
- Downtime was higher.
- Output was below budget.

These are lagging metrics.

They tell us the result, not necessarily the events and circumstances that created it.

Lunar must go considerably further.

It must investigate the underlying operational events, relationships, conditions and decisions that contributed to those results.

Consider this example.

A director walks into the office in the morning and says:

"Good morning, Luna. How was performance yesterday?"

I do not want Luna to respond by simply reading out yesterday's KPIs.

I want her to understand the operational story.

For example:

"Morning. Yesterday finished below plan, but there are a couple of things worth looking at before we judge the overall performance.

"One of the biggest contributors was Line 3. It ran Product A, which is normally produced more economically on Line 1. The product's speed, labour requirement and material consumption made it a less suitable fit for Line 3.

"That contributed to lower output and a higher conversion cost.

"There was also a changeover issue in the afternoon, which added further lost time.

"So the problem wasn't simply that the factory ran inefficiently. Part of the problem was the way production was allocated across the available lines.

"I'd look at the production plan and product-to-line allocation before treating this as a general efficiency problem."

This is an illustrative example, not a prescribed answer or a set of facts to invent.

The important point is that Luna should be capable of discovering this type of explanation when the evidence supports it.

The same headline performance result could have been created by completely different underlying circumstances.

For example:

- An unsuitable product was allocated to a particular production line.
- A product mix changed the economics of production.
- A particular machine was responsible for a disproportionate amount of downtime.
- A shift consistently performed differently from other shifts.
- A raw material variation affected yield or process performance.
- A planned maintenance activity was not completed.
- A production schedule created excessive changeovers.
- A staffing arrangement affected line capability.
- A machine was technically available but operating below its effective capacity.
- A particular product or customer order created unusually high waste.
- A combination of several individually small factors created a significant financial loss.

Lunar must be capable of investigating these relationships.

**The objective is to explain the events that created the performance, not simply report the performance itself.**

## The distinction

We need to build an analytical brain capable of moving through several levels:

1. What happened?
2. Where and when did it happen?
3. What changed?
4. What conditions were present?
5. What other events or variables were associated with it?
6. Which factors appear to have contributed?
7. What is the likely underlying explanation?
8. What evidence supports that explanation?
9. What is the operational and financial consequence?
10. What should we investigate or do next?

Do not assume that the first recorded reason is the true reason.

A downtime record might say "operator issue".

That is a description or recorded reason, not necessarily a root cause.

Lunar should be able to investigate whether the actual circumstances involved training, machine design, poor material presentation, an unsuitable process, inconsistent settings, unclear work instructions or something else.

It must distinguish the recorded explanation from the evidence-based explanation.

It must also recognise when the evidence is insufficient.

Never invent a root cause simply to provide a convincing answer.

# 3. THE ADAPTIVE ANALYTICAL BRAIN

I want you to design and implement a sophisticated analytical intelligence that can discover patterns and relationships rather than simply execute a fixed collection of reports.

Think of this as an adaptive manufacturing and business intelligence engine.

It should combine:

- Statistical analysis.
- Pattern recognition.
- Event analysis.
- Temporal analysis.
- Relationship discovery.
- Root-cause investigation.
- Financial impact analysis.
- Predictive analysis where justified.
- Historical comparison.
- Contextual business knowledge.
- Continuous learning from validated findings.

It should be able to examine information from different perspectives and determine which analytical methods are appropriate.

Do not build a system that blindly runs every possible statistical test on every dataset.

It needs to understand the type of data available, the business question, the structure of the operation and the relationships that might matter.

It should select appropriate analytical methods, test assumptions and explain limitations.

## 3.1 Pattern discovery

The engine should actively search for:

- Repeating patterns.
- Unusual variations.
- Trends and changes over time.
- Relationships between products and production lines.
- Differences between shifts, teams and operators where appropriate and authorised.
- Machine and equipment performance patterns.
- Product-specific losses.
- Process-specific losses.
- Changeover patterns.
- Material-related variations.
- Yield and waste relationships.
- Labour and productivity relationships.
- Cost differences between production routes.
- Interactions between multiple variables.
- Conditions that repeatedly precede a loss or failure.
- Emerging patterns that were not previously recognised.

It must be able to identify patterns that are not immediately obvious when looking at individual reports.

For example, it might discover that:

- A particular product consistently performs worse on one production line.
- The same machine causes a disproportionate amount of downtime when running a specific product.
- A certain shift has higher waste, but only when producing particular product families.
- Changeovers take significantly longer when production moves between certain product combinations.
- A factory's apparent efficiency loss is actually being driven by production mix.
- A small number of recurring events account for a large proportion of financial losses.

These are examples of analytical capabilities, not fixed assumptions.

## 3.2 Contextual relationship analysis

The engine must understand that operational variables do not exist in isolation.

For example:

Product → Production line → Machine configuration → Shift → Operator capability → Production speed → Yield → Waste → Output → Cost.

It should be capable of exploring relationships across these connected dimensions.

It must also distinguish between:

- Directly measured relationships.
- Statistical associations.
- Plausible contributing factors.
- Supported causal explanations.
- Confirmed root causes.

Correlation is not proof of causation.

Where evidence is insufficient, Luna should explain what is known, what is suspected and what additional information would help establish the cause.

## 3.3 Financial intelligence

This is particularly important.

Lunar should understand the commercial consequences of operational events.

Where sufficient information exists, it should translate operational performance into financial impact.

Examples include:

- Cost per unit.
- Cost per production hour.
- Conversion cost.
- Labour cost.
- Material losses.
- Yield losses.
- Waste costs.
- Lost contribution.
- Changeover costs.
- Product-to-line cost differences.
- Cost of poor quality.
- Cost of downtime.
- Financial impact of poor scheduling.
- Cost of underutilised capacity.

It must distinguish between actual recorded financial losses, calculated estimates and potential opportunity values.

Do not invent selling prices, margins, standard costs or financial assumptions.

Use authorised business data and clearly identify the assumptions behind any calculation.

## 3.4 Statistical and analytical methods

Build an appropriate analytical capability using reliable code and statistical libraries.

Consider methods including:

- Descriptive statistics.
- Distributions and variation.
- Standard deviation and variance.
- Percentiles and quartiles.
- Pareto analysis.
- Trend analysis.
- Moving averages.
- Time-series analysis.
- Correlation.
- Regression.
- Multivariable analysis.
- Hypothesis testing.
- Confidence intervals.
- Statistical significance.
- Process capability.
- Process stability and control charts.
- Outlier detection.
- Anomaly detection.
- Segmentation and clustering where appropriate.
- Shift, product, line and machine comparisons.
- Event sequence analysis.
- Before-and-after comparisons.
- Predictive modelling where sufficient data exists.

These are capabilities to select from, not a requirement to run every test on every dataset.

The engine must check data suitability and assumptions.

For example, process capability calculations should not be presented as meaningful when the underlying process stability or data assumptions are not satisfied.

Statistical significance and operational significance are not the same thing.

A statistically significant difference may be commercially insignificant. A relatively small statistical difference may nevertheless represent a substantial financial loss.

Luna should understand that distinction.

# 4. CONTINUOUS ABSORPTION AND ANALYSIS OF INFORMATION

Excel is our first major proving ground.

However, I do not want an Excel analysis product.

I want a platform capable of absorbing and understanding information from every relevant source connected to Lunar.

Potential sources include:

- Excel workbooks.
- CSV files.
- Documents and PDFs.
- Production reports.
- ERP systems.
- MES systems.
- SharePoint.
- Production databases.
- Maintenance systems.
- Quality systems.
- Machine data.
- Downtime records.
- Factory observations.
- Photographs and supporting evidence.
- Existing Lunar investigations.
- Measurements and studies.
- Reports and action histories.
- Future connected applications and systems.

The architecture must be source-independent.

Excel is simply the first entry point.

## 4.1 Initial ingestion

When a user uploads a workbook or connects a source, Lunar should automatically begin understanding it.

This should include:

- Identifying sheets and tables.
- Understanding headers and fields.
- Identifying dates, units, values and categories.
- Recognising data types.
- Identifying potential relationships between sheets.
- Identifying possible business entities.
- Understanding the time periods represented.
- Assessing data quality.
- Identifying missing and duplicate information.
- Detecting inconsistent naming.
- Identifying potential analytical opportunities.
- Running appropriate initial analysis.
- Producing a persistent record of findings.

For Excel, the engine must be able to handle complex workbooks rather than simply read the first sheet.

It should investigate relationships across sheets and tables where appropriate.

Large workbooks must not be sent wholesale to an LLM.

Use appropriate file storage, parsing, processing and analytical infrastructure.

## 4.2 Ongoing absorption

When a connected source changes, Lunar should detect the change and determine what needs updating.

It should not need a user to ask:

"Can you analyse the latest data?"

It should already be doing that work.

The engine should support:

- Initial full analysis.
- Event-driven updates.
- Incremental processing.
- Scheduled analysis.
- Historical reconciliation.
- Detection of changed or new information.
- Updating affected findings.
- Identifying new patterns.
- Identifying when previously valid findings are no longer current.

Do not repeatedly reprocess unchanged information unnecessarily.

## 4.3 Proposed analytical schedule

Design an appropriate processing architecture that can support:

**On initial upload or connection**

Perform initial data understanding and appropriate analysis.

**When new information arrives**

Process changes and update affected knowledge and findings.

**Near real-time where practical**

Identify important emerging events or changes.

**Nightly**

Reconcile data, conduct deferred calculations, update historical comparisons and refresh analytical findings.

**Weekly**

Look for broader patterns, recurring relationships and changes across the operation.

**Monthly and longer-term**

Examine longer historical trends, changing relationships, persistent losses and the effectiveness of previously identified improvement opportunities.

The schedule should be configurable and appropriate to source volume, business value, processing cost and technical constraints.

Implement proper background processing, queues, retries, idempotency and monitoring.

The interface must show whether information is:

- Processing.
- Up to date.
- Partially analysed.
- Awaiting processing.
- Failed.
- Stale.
- Incomplete.

Do not pretend the analysis is current when it is not.

# 5. PERSISTENT ANALYTICAL KNOWLEDGE

The analytical brain must retain what it discovers.

It must not rely on a temporary AI conversation or assume the model remembers previous API calls.

Use the appropriate persistent infrastructure, including Supabase and suitable object storage where appropriate.

Store structured findings, relationships, analytical results and provenance.

Each finding should retain relevant information such as:

- Customer and site.
- Related department, line, machine, product or process.
- Source and source version.
- Relevant date range.
- Analytical method.
- Parameters and assumptions.
- Sample size.
- Results.
- Confidence and uncertainty.
- Limitations.
- Financial implications where established.
- Date of analysis.
- Processing version.
- Whether the finding has been reviewed or validated.

Luna must be able to retrieve this knowledge when answering questions.

For example, if a director asks about yesterday's performance, Luna should not need to analyse an entire workbook from scratch.

She should retrieve the relevant findings, understand the events and relationships, and formulate an explanation.

If additional analysis is required, she should request it from the analytical engine.

The model should not be expected to perform complex calculations by reading huge amounts of raw data.

# 6. THE LEARNING LOOP

I want Lunar to become more capable through experience.

Not by pretending the underlying AI model automatically retrains itself every time someone uses it.

I mean an explicit, governed learning system.

It should retain knowledge about:

- Which analytical methods have been useful.
- What types of data those methods require.
- What assumptions they depend on.
- What patterns they have identified.
- Which findings were subsequently validated.
- Which hypotheses proved incorrect.
- Which methods were unsuitable.
- Which analytical approaches worked well in similar circumstances.
- Which relationships repeatedly appear in particular manufacturing environments.

For example, if the engine discovers that product-to-line allocation is a significant source of cost variation in one business, it should retain the analytical method and the conditions under which that analysis was useful
